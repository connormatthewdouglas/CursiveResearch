## The Challenged Claim

Chapter 01 §2.1 ("Linux Defaults Are Optimized for Compatibility, Not
Performance") states, as the first concrete evidence for First Principle #1:

> "TCP socket buffers default to 212KB (appropriate for 1990s modem speeds)."

This is not a minor aside. §2.1 calls the resulting gap "the foundational
opportunity CursiveOS exploits," and the chapter's own Research-master
assessment canonizes the section: the five first principles are "rock-solid,"
"now canon," and "should be quoted verbatim in future white-paper revisions and
Copper prompt templates." So this is plausibly the single most load-bearing
technical sentence in the strategy corpus — which is exactly why it should be
correct, and it is not.

## Why the Claim Is Overstated / Wrong

Two distinct errors are compressed into one sentence.

### 1. "TCP socket buffers default to 212KB" conflates a manual-override ceiling with the default.

The 212 KB figure is `net.core.rmem_max` / `net.core.wmem_max`, whose default is
**212992 bytes** on modern Linux. That sysctl is *not* the buffer an ordinary
connection gets. It is the upper bound that applies **only** to applications that
explicitly call `setsockopt(SO_RCVBUF/SO_SNDBUF)` and thereby opt out of
autotuning.

The default path — any socket that does *not* manually set its buffer — is
governed by `net.ipv4.tcp_rmem` / `tcp_wmem`, each a `min default max` triple.
Typical modern defaults are roughly `tcp_rmem = 4096 131072 6291456` and
`tcp_wmem = 4096 16384 4194304`, i.e. the kernel **dynamically grows the window
up to ~4–6 MB** as the path's bandwidth-delay product requires. This is TCP
receive-buffer autotuning (`tcp_moderate_rcvbuf`, enabled by default), shipped
and on-by-default since the 2.6.x series (~2004–2006). There is no static 212 KB
ceiling on a normal connection.

Sources:
- Linux `tcp(7)` man page — documents `tcp_rmem`/`tcp_wmem` triples and
  `tcp_moderate_rcvbuf` autotuning: https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/tcp.7.html
- Red Hat Enterprise Linux 10, "Tuning TCP connections for high throughput" —
  "the maximum value for the TCP receive buffer cannot exceed
  `net.core.rmem_max`" and the `tcp_rmem` triple is min/default/max for
  autotuning:
  https://docs.redhat.com/en/documentation/red_hat_enterprise_linux/10/html/network_troubleshooting_and_performance_tuning/tuning-tcp-connections-for-high-throughput
- ESnet Fasterdata Linux tuning guide — modern kernels autotune; manual buffer
  tuning "usually will not improve networking performance considerably":
  https://fasterdata.es.net/host-tuning/linux/

### 2. "(appropriate for 1990s modem speeds)" is quantitatively off by 2–4 orders of magnitude.

Maximum single-stream throughput ≈ window ÷ RTT. Even taking the 212 KB ceiling
at face value (212992 bytes ≈ 1.70 Mbit):

| RTT | Throughput supported by a 212 KB window |
| --- | --- |
| 100 ms (transcontinental) | ~17 Mbit/s |
| 10 ms (regional) | ~170 Mbit/s |
| 1 ms (LAN/datacenter) | ~1.7 Gbit/s |

A 1990s V.90 modem ran at 0.056 Mbit/s. A 212 KB window therefore supports
roughly **300× a modem even at a slow 100 ms RTT, and ~30,000× at LAN
latencies** — not "modem speeds." And on the *actual* default path (autotuning
to ~6 MB), the supported throughput is ~480 Mbit/s at 100 ms RTT and multiple
Gbit/s on a LAN. The "1990s modem" framing is rhetorically vivid and
numerically false.

## The Corpus's Own Validated Evidence Already Contradicts §2.1

This is not only an external-literature problem. The corpus's most rigorous
network finding directly refutes the §2.1 framing:

- CHANGELOG 2026-06-16 ("Real-Path A/B Overturns the Stack-Delta Magnitude"):
  the real ≤1GbE A/B shows the entire network win is the **CUBIC→BBR

  on ordinary ≤1GbE links.
- VALIDATION.md "Chapter 16 / network headline" (Validated, 2026-06-16): same
  conclusion — public claim is "switch to BBR"; do **not** credit buffer tuning
  with a magnitude.

In other words, the corpus has already measured that the TCP-buffer "gap" §2.1
holds up as the flagship example of wasted performance contributes ≈0% on real
links, and that the real win comes from a *different* mechanism (congestion
control), not buffer size. §2.1 still presents the buffer default as the
headline proof of the thesis, and the Research-master note still tells future
editors to quote it verbatim.

## Scope of the Challenge (What This Does NOT Claim)

- It does **not** refute First Principle #1 as a whole. "Linux defaults favor
  compatibility/safety over peak throughput" is directionally defensible, and the
  *other* §2.1 examples are sounder: CPU governors defaulting to non-performance
  modes and GPU idle-frequency behavior are consistent with the corpus's
  validated cold-start gains (VALIDATION.md "Chapter 16 / cold-start").
- The challenge is narrow and specific: the **TCP-buffer sentence** is both
  technically incorrect (autotuning, not a 212 KB default) and rhetorically
  overstated ("1990s modem speeds"), and it should not be the canonical,
  quote-verbatim evidence for the market/moat thesis.
